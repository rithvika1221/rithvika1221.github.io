A database never trusts its memory. Before it changes anything, it writes the change down in a log, and other machines replay that log, one record at a time, until they catch up.

I've spent this past year studying how long that catching up takes. This is my own log, replayed from the first record. Every chapter also hides a query, if you want the details.

It starts with a violin.

In fifth grade I picked up the violin and learned a word I'd keep running into for the rest of this story: arco, Italian for “with the bow.”

Six years of orchestra taught me something no class did. Getting better isn't one big leap. It's the same short passage, replayed slowly, then a little faster, until your hands remember it without you. Small changes, applied in order. I didn't know it yet, but that's exactly how a database recovers.

Orchestra also showed me what a commit feels like: sixty musicians, one downbeat, and everyone's part has to land together or none of it works.

Then I became the one teaching.

Every week for more than four years, I've helped younger kids learn Telugu, my family's language, at Mana Samskriti and Telugu Bharati, a community I've been part of since 2018.

A six-year-old doesn't care how fluent you are. They care whether you can get the idea from your head into theirs. Teaching made me notice the gap between knowing something and someone else having it, and how much patience it takes to close. Along the way I earned Washington's Seal of Biliteracy in Telugu.

That gap shows up in almost everything I've built since.

Code turned out to be another way to tell stories.

At Girls Who Code I made shapes move across a screen with p5.js, and I was hooked on typing something and watching it come alive.

I started entering FBLA coding competitions. My first big project was GradeMaster, a GPA tracker with AI features, refined through rounds of feedback from real students. A year later I built Mythic Pathways, a branching-story app where readers choose their way through myths, with narration and a way for other authors to publish their own. That year I took 1st in Washington and 3rd at Nationals.

On the side I was grinding USACO problems, and eventually qualified for the Gold division. Stories and algorithms felt like opposite interests. They're really the same skill: deciding what happens next.

Some problems aren't technical.

Around me, people my age were struggling with stress and anxiety, and many weren't getting support. Awareness posters weren't enough.

So in June 2024 I started Mind Mission, a student-led mental-health initiative. We ran workshops on managing stress and balancing life, and I built its website so counselors, helplines and trusted resources were one tap away instead of buried. It grew from one school to the whole district and into community partnerships, reaching more than 500 students.

I also wrote and published a book, Finding Strength Within, for teens who'd rather read than talk; it's been handed out at workshops and libraries. I became Western Regional Director for Address Teen Stress, and sat on my school's Counseling Advisory Council to help shape what support looks like from the inside.

Same gap as teaching Telugu: the help existed. It just wasn't reaching the people who needed it.

Then I met the messy data the real world runs on.

At the University of Washington's SEAL Laboratory, engineers write energy-savings reports for local industry, and much of the data work was being done by hand.

I wrote JavaScript and Google Apps Script tools that searched, cleaned and summarized the assessment data, and they ended up supporting more than 50 professional reports. It was the first time code I wrote saved someone else hours.

The next summer I interned at Rove Miles, a Y Combinator-backed travel startup, designing algorithms to find the best-value ways to redeem airline miles and co-writing a paper on how airlines distribute fares. The same summer I joined Harvard's HUVTSP program, doing market research and product strategy with startup teams. I learned that a working program is only half the job. The other half is understanding who it's for.

’25–26LSN 0/7E9–7EArecord 6 of 7

I went looking for the gap inside the database itself.

Almost every app I'd built sat on PostgreSQL. So I asked a question about it: when you copy a database to other machines, why does the copy sometimes fall behind, and can you see it coming?

In PostgreSQL's logical replication, the main server streams every change to its replicas, which apply those changes in order. When writes arrive faster than a replica can apply them, a backlog builds. That backlog is replication lag, and when it grows unnoticed, people read stale data.

I designed and ran 29 controlled experiments on Microsoft Azure, varying the workload and recording what happened on both machines. Then I trained a machine-learning model that predicts replication lag from ordinary system metrics, so operators can see trouble coming before their readers do.

My paper has been accepted for publication in the National High School Journal of Science. The experiment scripts and results are on GitHub.

Studying PostgreSQL that closely pulled me into building it. I started compiling the source, testing other people's patches and reviewing them on the pgsql-hackers mailing list, where the database is designed in public. I reported bugs I found in its internals. Then code I wrote was accepted into PostgreSQL itself, and I'm credited by name in the official commits.

One of the most widely used databases in the world now has a few lines from a high school student in Bothell. I still find that hard to believe.

And then the story bent back to the violin.

This year I started working with Dr. Roger B. Dannenberg at Carnegie Mellon, a pioneer of computer music. His group has a sound-synthesis system that new students could no longer even install.

It's called Arco. The same word from my first orchestra rehearsal: with the bow.

I worked through the build piece by piece and fixed more than fifteen bugs that were stopping it from installing. Every fix was reviewed and merged into the official codebase. Then I wrote a step-by-step setup guide so the next student researcher can start making sound on day one.

A bowed A (440 Hz), synthesized in your browser from a sawtooth wave, a filter, a slow attack and a little vibrato: the same kind of building blocks a synthesis system like Arco patches together.

A setup guide is a small thing. It's also the clearest version of what I keep doing: noticing the gap between what someone knows and what someone else can reach, and building the bridge.

Replica caught up. Still writing.
