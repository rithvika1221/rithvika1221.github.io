I've spent this past year studying how long that catching up takes. This is my own log, replayed from the first record.

2019record 1 of 7

It starts with a violin.

In fifth grade I picked up the violin and learned a word I'd keep running into for the rest of this story: arco, Italian for “with the bow.”

Six years of orchestra taught me something no class did. Getting better isn't one big leap. It's the same short passage, replayed slowly, then a little faster, until your hands remember it without you. Small changes, applied in order. I didn't know it yet, but that's exactly how a database recovers.

Then I became the one teaching.

Every week for more than four years, I've helped younger kids learn Telugu, my family's language, at Mana Samskriti and Telugu Bharati.

A six-year-old doesn't care how fluent you are. They care whether you can get the idea from your head into theirs. Teaching made me notice the gap between knowing something and someone else having it, and how much patience it takes to close.

That gap shows up in almost everything I've built since.

’25–26record 6 of 7

I went looking for the gap inside the database itself.

Almost every app I'd built sat on PostgreSQL. So I asked a question about it: when you copy a database to other machines, why does the copy sometimes fall behind, and can you see it coming?

In PostgreSQL's logical replication, the main server streams every change to its replicas, which apply those changes in order. When writes arrive faster than a replica can apply them, a backlog builds. That backlog is replication lag, and when it grows unnoticed, people read stale data.

I designed and ran 29 controlled experiments on Microsoft Azure, varying the workload and recording what happened on both machines. Then I trained a machine-learning model that predicts replication lag from ordinary system metrics, so operators can see trouble coming before their readers do.

My paper has been accepted for publication in the National High School Journal of Science.

Studying PostgreSQL that closely pulled me into building it. I started compiling the source, testing other people's patches and reviewing them on the pgsql-hackers mailing list, where the database is designed in public. I reported bugs I found in its internals. Then code I wrote was accepted into PostgreSQL itself, and I'm credited by name in the official commits.

One of the most widely used databases in the world now has a few lines from a high school student in Bothell. I still find that hard to believe.

2026record 7 of 7

And then the story bent back to the violin.

This year I started working with Dr. Roger B. Dannenberg at Carnegie Mellon, a pioneer of computer music. His group has a sound-synthesis system that new students could no longer even install.

It's called Arco. The same word from my first orchestra rehearsal: with the bow.

I worked through the build piece by piece and fixed more than fifteen bugs that were stopping it from installing. Every fix was reviewed and merged into the official codebase. Then I wrote a step-by-step setup guide so the next student researcher can start making sound on day one.

A bowed A (440 Hz), synthesized in your browser from a sawtooth wave, a filter, a slow attack and a little vibrato: the same kind of building blocks a synthesis system like Arco patches together.

A setup guide is a small thing. It's also the clearest version of what I keep doing: noticing the gap between what someone knows and what someone else can reach, and building the bridge.
